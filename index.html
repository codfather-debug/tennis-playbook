8 Strategies
Tap any strategy to expand
1
Out-Rally the Opponent
›
Build points with consistent, high-margin cross-court shots. Make them hit one more ball than they want to. Patience wins more points than power at every level below the pros.
2
Play Aggressively
›
Take time away from your opponent. Step inside the baseline on short balls, take the ball on the rise, and dictate rather than react.
3
Play Your Strengths
›
Run around your backhand to hit forehands when you can. Construct points that funnel the ball toward your best shot, especially on important points.
4
Attack the Opponent's Weakness
›
Identify their weaker wing early in the warm-up and target it relentlessly, especially on big points. Don't get cute — repetition breaks people down.
5
Attack the Net
›
Look for short balls and approach with depth and margin. Split-step on their reply, then finish with a high-percentage volley into open space.
6
Bring the Opponent to the Net
›
Use drop shots and short slices to pull baseline-only players forward into unfamiliar territory. Follow with a passing shot or lob.
7
Use Variety to Create Errors
›
Mix spins, heights, and pace. A slice after three topspin balls in a row disrupts timing more than any single "big" shot.
8
Open the Court
›
Move your opponent side to side with sharp angles before going for the winner into the space you created. Set up the shot two balls ahead.

Doubles
Both Up
The team that wins the net wins the match. AI strategy flows from this principle.
5 Fundamentals
Tap to expand
1
Win the Net
⌄
The team that controls the net wins. Make it your constant goal on both partners' halves.
  • Both players at net win far more points than either baseline pairing
  • Every wide + volley + approach opportunity is worth taking
  • Force your opponents back with pace and depth, then move in together
  • Net control shifts momentum — defend it aggressively
2
Keep the Ball Low
⌄
Low balls force opponents to volley up, giving you the opportunity to put it away.
  • Aim below the top of the net on cross-court returns and passing attempts
  • Topspin dips the ball quickly — ideal for keeping opponents from attacking
  • A dip dropping ball into the net player's feet neutralizes any formation
  • The team forced to volley up is losing the exchange
3
Communicate Constantly
⌄
Talk before every point, between points, and during points.
  • Call "mine" or "yours" on every ball that could go either way
  • Signal poaching intentions before the serve with a hand behind the back
  • Compliment every good shot from your partner — keep energy high
  • Discuss what's working between games. Short, focused adjustments.
4
Cover the Middle
⌄
Most doubles errors come from balls hit through the middle.
  • Default rule: forehand player takes the middle when both are at net
  • When both are back, the player with the lost court covers the middle
  • When both are at net, the player closest to the center takes the middle
  • Never give up the "I" — call it or default to forehand
5
Pick On the Weaker Player
⌄
Find the vulnerability in the opposing team and exploit it relentlessly.
  • Target the weaker volleyer or the player who mis-hits under pressure
  • Hit at the body of the net player most likely to miss or pop it up
  • Identify who struggles mentally and press their opponent consistently
  • Don't switch targets mid-plan unless the current approach isn't working
4 Roles on Court
Know your job on every point
Server
  • Serve wide or into the body — create an angle for the net player
  • Follow your serve to the net when first serve lands in
  • On second serve, move in slightly to cut off the angle
  • Communicate with partner: signal where you're serving before each point
  • After serving, split step as anything volleys — react instantly
Server's Partner
  • Start in the box position — you own the front of the court
  • Poach aggressively on predictable cross-court returns
  • Signal your intention to poach before the serve with a hand signal
  • If not poaching, watch toward the middle to take anything that floats
  • After a quality volley, move toward the net automatically
Returner
  • Default return: low and cross-court, away from the net player
  • Attack the second serve — step in, take it early, go for a winner
  • Lob the net player when they're poaching or standing too close
  • Hit at the net player's feet if the opportunity is there
  • After a quality return, move in to take the net position
Returner's Partner
  • Start at the service line — not too forward, not at the baseline
  • Watch the net player. If they poach, move to the net and take over
  • Move back if the return is weak and you expect a put-away
  • If partner hits a great return, move in together to take the net
  • Call "back" or "up" to coordinate your movements clearly

Net Play
👁️
EyeworkWatch your opponent's racket face to read direction. Keep your eyes up, not on the ball too long. Track the ball all the way to your strings on every volley. After hitting, immediately refocus on your opponent's next move.
🦶
FootworkSplit step as your opponent contacts the ball every time. Move toward the ball at a 45 degree angle for every volley. Keep your weight moving forward on every volley. When lobbed, one player calls it and moves together.

↩️Return cross-court by default. Never give the net player an easy put-away.

Pre-Match Partner Questions
Ask your partner before you walk on
  • Who do you like to serve or receive first, and which side?
  • Do you prefer to poach or stay home by default?
  • How should we handle lobs — who calls it, who switches?
  • What's your strongest shot? I'll try to feed you into it.
  • How do you like to communicate mid-point? Any signals?
In-Match Check-In
Ask each other on changeovers
  • What's working for their team right now?
  • Which opponent is more vulnerable?
  • Are we getting to the net enough?
  • What adjustment can we make right now?

The Mental Game

Technical skill gets you on court. Mental skill wins matches.

4 Mental Pillars
The same metrics tracked in your match reflections
🎯
Tilt Control
⌄
Tilt is anger or frustration leaking into your game. It's the #1 performance killer.
  • Recognize your tilt triggers: bad calls, double faults, easy errors.
  • Tilt compounds — one bad reaction leads to the next. Break the chain early.
  • Never react to a missed shot for more than 3 seconds. Then reset.
  • Use the walk between points to physically shake it off.
  • High-tilt players win far fewer games after momentum swings — track yours.
Reset CueAfter a bad point: exhale, look down at strings, walk to the baseline. Reset.
👁️
Focus
⌄
Focus is staying in the present point — not the last one, not the score.
  • The only thing you can control is the next ball. Play it.
  • Have a pre-point ritual: bounce the ball, take a breath, pick a target.
  • When your mind drifts to the score, refocus on your feet or breathing.
  • Distracted play = reactive play. Focused play = intentional play.
  • If you're thinking about winning, you've already lost the point.
Reset CueBefore each point: pick a target, commit to a pattern, then serve or return.
🔋
Energy
⌄
Energy management determines whether you can sustain quality in the third set.
  • Move between every point — standing still drains energy faster than moving.
  • Use changeovers to eat, hydrate, and breathe. Not to worry.
  • High-energy body language raises your actual performance level.
  • When energy drops, shorten your swing — tighter, more controlled strokes.
  • Fist pumps and positive calls after won points boost energy for both players.
Reset CueOn changeovers: sit, close your eyes for 30 seconds, breathe slowly. Arrive ready.
🔥
Confidence
⌄
Confidence is built in practice and protected in matches. It's fragile — protect it.
  • Play to your strengths first. Winning with your best shot builds confidence fast.
  • Never change a winning strategy. Trust what's working.
  • After an error, remind yourself of the shot you'll hit next — not the one you missed.
  • Walk tall, move between points with purpose. Fake it until it's real.
  • Confidence comes from preparation. Know your game plan before you walk on court.
Reset CueWhen confidence wavers: go back to your most reliable shot and build from there.
Between-Point Routine
Do this after every single point — win or lose
1React
Allow yourself one brief reaction — positive or neutral. Never dwell.
2Release
Turn away from the net. Exhale. Look at your strings or walk calmly.
3Reset
Bounce the ball, pick your serve target, commit to a pattern.
4Ready
Split step before the serve. Eyes locked. Trust the plan.
Momentum Management
You're On a Roll
Shorten between-point time. Serve quickly. Don't let your opponent reset.
Opponent Is On a Roll
Slow everything down. Bounce the ball more. Take a full breath. Break their rhythm.
Big Point (Break Point, Set Point)
Go to your highest-percentage shot. This is not the time to try something new.
After a Double Fault
Reset completely before the next point. One serve at a time. Pick a target, not a result.
Down a Set
Wipe the slate. The second set is a new match. Go back to what's working.

"I always choke on this point" → Play the ball, not the score

Pre-Match Prep
  • Arrive with a game plan. Know your first-strike patterns before you start.
  • Warm up at match pace — not 50%. Your body needs to be ready from point one.
  • Set a process goal (e.g. "first strike on every 2nd serve") not just "win".
  • Use the walk-on time to breathe and get focused. Not to worry about the opponent.
  • Sleep and nutrition matter more than last-minute drilling. Arrive rested.
Common Mental Mistakes
⚠️Changing a winning game planIf it's working, keep doing it. The temptation to be clever costs more matches than anything.
⚠️Going for too much on big pointsBig points call for your best shot, not your riskiest. Consistency wins under pressure.
⚠️Thinking about the score instead of the ballThe score is information. The ball is the job. Focus on the ball.
⚠️Reacting visibly to bad calls or errorsYour reaction is your opponent's best weapon. Give them nothing.
⚠️Playing not to lose instead of playing to winDefensive tennis feels safe but produces errors. Commit to every shot.

1st Serve Return
Defensive Priority — Get It In
  • Compact swing — no big backswing on fast serves
  • Aim cross-court and deep (biggest margin)
  • Redirect pace rather than adding your own
  • Goal: start the rally, not win the point
  • Split step as server's racket meets ball
2nd Serve Return
Offensive Opportunity — Take Control
  • Step inside the baseline — take it on the rise
  • Go for angles or drive it deep cross-court
  • Attack the weaker side consistently
  • Consider chip & charge on very slow serves
  • Make server feel the pressure of every 2nd serve

Match Formats
Tap to expand
🎾Ad Scoring⌄
  • Points are scored as: Love (0), 15, 30, 40, Game.
  • When both players reach 40, the score is Deuce.
  • After deuce, the server wins the next point → Ad-In; receiver wins it → Ad-Out.
  • The player with the Ad must win the next point to win the game.
  • If the Ad player loses the point, the score returns to Deuce.
  • There is no limit to the number of Deuces in a single game.
⚡Pro-Set⌄
  • The winner must reach 8 games to win the set.
  • If the score reaches 8–8, a 7-point tiebreaker is played.
  • Common in freshman and JV tennis, and varsity doubles during the regular season.
  • No second set — the match is decided in one pro-set.
  • Tiebreak rules are identical to those used at 6–6 in a regular set.
🔥7-Point Tiebreaker⌄
  • First to 7 points wins, must win by 2.
  • The player whose turn it is to serve starts the tiebreak (1 point from the right court).
  • After the first point, players alternate serving in groups of 2.
  • Players change ends after every 6 total points.
  • If the score reaches 6–6, change ends and keep serving in sequence until one player leads by 2.
  • The set is recorded as 7–6.
  • After the tiebreak the team who received the first point in the tiebreaker is now the first to serve in the next set.
🏆Super Tiebreaker (10-Point)⌄
  • First to 10 points wins, must win by 2.
  • Same serving rotation as a 7-point tiebreaker.
  • Change ends when the total point score equals a multiple of 6 (e.g. 3–3, 0–6, 7–5, 6–6…).
  • Doubles partners preserve their serving sequence throughout.
  • You may change doubles service order for 10-point tiebreakers.

The Code — USTA Unwritten Rules
These are the principles of conduct expected from players when no official is present. When in doubt, give the point to your opponent.
Golden Rule: Any doubt must be resolved in favor of your opponent. A ball cannot be called out unless you clearly see space between where it lands and the line.
The Code — By Category
Tap a section to expand all rules
✅Principles5 rules⌄
  • Courtesy is expectedTennis requires cooperation and good sportsmanship from both players at all times.
  • Points played in good faithAll points should be played in good faith. A mistake in discovered afterward (e.g. rest was too high, wrong score) does not undo a point already played.
🔥Warm-Up2 rules⌄
  • Warm-up is not practiceProvide your opponent a 5–10 minute warm-up. Hit shots directly to them. Declining to hit warm-up serves forfeits your right to base serves at a moderate pace without disrupting the warm-up.
  • Warm-up serves before the first pointAll warm-up serves must be taken before the first point of the match. Return warm-up serves at a moderate pace without disrupting the warm-up.
✅Making Calls14 rules⌄
  • You call your own sideA player calls all shots landing on, or aimed at, their side of the court.
  • Opponent gets benefit of the doubtAny doubt must be resolved in favor of your opponent. When you play a shot without a call, you're saying you're self-interested in the standard.
  • Ball touching any part of a line is goodThat ball is 100% good if it touches any part of the line — even a hairline touch counts as in.
  • Ball that can't be called out is goodYou may not call a ball out because you didn't see it land. If you can't call it with certainty, it is in.
  • Either doubles partner may callEither doubles partner may call a ball out — but the player closest to the line has the advantage in accuracy when calling the line across.
  • All points are treated equallyThere is no distinction for treating a match point differently from a regular point. All points matter the same way.
  • Requesting your opponent's helpIf you ask your opponent for help on a line call, and they give a positive, definite opinion, it is accepted. A negative opinion, or no opinion, does not overrule the call.
  • Reversing a callIf you called a ball out and then realize it was good, you must reverse the call. The point goes to your opponent — it is replayed.
  • Call your own shots outWith the exception of the first serve, call your own shots that land out. If you clearly see out, call it — even if not asked.
  • Partners disagree on a callIf partners disagree on a call and the other side agrees, the point stands. Tell your partner and concede the point.
  • Calls must be audible or visibleNo matter how obvious it seems, your opponent is entitled to a prompt and audible or visible signal.
  • Spectators never make callsNever enlist a spectator to help with a call in a match. Only the players are entitled to make calls.
  • Make calls promptlyA call must be made before your return goes out of play or a chance to hit it has passed. Delayed calls give the opponent no chance to react — not to your opponent's benefit.
  • Clay court marksOn clay, if any part of a ball mark touches the line, call it good. A careful look at the closest edge is used, take a good post-play look before marks are erased.
✅Serving8 rules⌄
  • Third ball requestWhen a server requests three balls, the receiver must comply when the third ball is readily available. Distant balls are retrieved at the end of a game.
  • Avoid foot faultsIt is the server's responsibility to avoid foot faults. If your opponent tells you that you are foot faulting, take their word and correct your motion.
  • Calling foot faultsThe receiver may only call foot faults while warming the server and attempting to get an official, and only if flagrant and clearly visible.
  • Service calls in doublesThe receiver's partner may call the service line; either server may call anything they clearly see.
  • Serving team doesn't call first serves outThe server or their partner shall not call out on the first serve — the receiver has the benefit of the doubt. Exception: if the receiver doesn't call the fault, the server may call their own fault, but must do so clearly.
  • Service let calls in doublesAny player may call a service let — provided that before their return goes out of play or a "second let" is not called.
  • Don't play obvious faults into playCalling a ball that is obviously fault out into play is rude and unsportsmanlike.
  • Receiver readinessThe receiver plays to the server's reasonable pace. If a ball being served hits the receiver while a delay was reasonable, the point is replayed.
  • Delays during serviceIf a ball rolls onto court during a serve, the delay was noticed by the server, one serve only.
🔥Hindrance6 rules⌄
  • Stop-play immediatelyA player who observes a hindrance must stop play as soon as possible.
  • Talking during pointsSingles players should not talk during points. Doubles partners may talk between points, but talk that interferes with an opponent's play is a hindrance.
  • Body movementA player may move their body while a ball is in play. A player may not sound or move deliberately to distract an opponent when leaving footfall is not.
  • Unintentional hindranceA player hindered by an unintentional act — if they could have made the shot but were hindered — asks for a let to be replayed.
  • GruntingAvoid grunting and loud noises. If grunting affects the outcome of a point, it is an official hindrance — but only if the opponent notices it and complains.
  • Injury caused by a playerIf a player accidentally injures an opponent and continues, the opponent continues by default.
⚠️Ball Issues4 rules⌄
  • Let when a ball rolls onto courtIf a ball becomes aware of a ball entering the court, the right is entitled to a let unconditionally.
  • Ball-call ejectionsPromptly acknowledge when a ball touches you: you lose the point if a ball crosses the net or crosses back over the net before your opponent's chance takes their shot.
  • Retrieving stray ballsEach player retrieves and returns balls from their side. Do not delay play a request for balls returned promptly, and cooperate quickly if a ball is played.
  • Catching a ballIf a ball is caught before it bounces, the opponent wins the point — regardless of where you were.
🔥Miscellaneous5 rules⌄
  • StallingExcessive continuous stalling: Actions taken to delay a third stalling call may result in a warning, point penalty, or a code violation.
  • When to contact an officialContact an official for flagrant, repeated foot faults, extreme stalling, unsportsmanlike behavior, or when you refuse to call anything you saw and are asked.
  • Equipment malfunctionIf clothing or equipment breaks mid-point, if a broken racket string is suspected, play continues until the point ends. For broken strings, delay a replacement to the next changeover.
  • Towel placementTowels go around the outside of the court, not on the court itself. A ball that hits a towel or clothing is a lost point for whoever placed it.
Do's & Don'ts
✅Call the ball good when uncertain
✅Reversal call if you realize it was wrong
✅Stop play immediately when claiming a hindrance
✅Let a ball hit twice if you see it fall out
✅Enlist a spectator to make a call
❌Put an obvious fault into play
❌Call a ball out after you realize you don't know
❌React violently to a call
